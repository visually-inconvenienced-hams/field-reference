Using and printing this book

Master and section editions

This field reference is maintained as one master source. Use the master selector to choose the sections you want.

Download a selected PDF for fixed large-print output, or a real Word document for editing. Individual section files are supplied in the complete kit.

The complete phonetic alphabet has its own single page. Morse letters, numbers, punctuation and procedural signals use separate tables.

Master content changes take effect in newly rebuilt editions. A Word or PDF already downloaded remains a snapshot of its revision.

Print at actual size on letter paper, double-sided with long-edge flipping. Test one sheet before lamination.

Print and bind

Print one test sheet first. Choose US Letter, actual size or 100 percent, portrait, double-sided, and flip on the long edge. Disable booklet and multiple-pages-per-sheet options.

The inner margin is 1.1 inches; the outer margin is 0.6 inch. Odd and even pages mirror for left-edge comb binding.

Standard Rite in the Rain printer paper uses laser printing or photocopying, not inkjet. Confirm your exact paper and printer compatibility.

Read the test in field lighting before printing the book. Test toner adhesion, duplex alignment, pouch heat and punching on scrap first.

Use matte pouches if possible to reduce glare. Check the laminator, pouch and paper instructions for heat compatibility.

A letter-size sheet normally needs a larger pouch to leave a sealed border. Do not trim through that border. Check the finished width against the comb punch and binding capacity.

Keep punches in the reserved margin. Holes through the sealed area can admit water; test one finished sheet before making the rest.

Morse symbols sent as sequences

Percent has no separate core character. ITU procedure sends the characters 0/0. Per thousand uses 0/00. These contain normal character gaps.

For 2 percent, send 2-0/0. A minute sign after a number uses the apostrophe signal once; a seconds sign uses it twice.

Do not invent a joined signal for a symbol with no agreed assignment. In plain amateur correspondence, spelling its name may avoid ambiguity.

References: Morse code

Morse timing and distress

A dit is one time unit. A dah is three units. Gaps are one unit within a character, three between characters, and seven between words.

SOS is dit dit dit dah dah dah dit dit dit as one continuous distress signal. There are no S O S letter gaps. Use it for distress, not for a routine test.

For the PARIS speed standard, dit duration in milliseconds is 1200 divided by words per minute. At 20 WPM, a dit lasts 60 milliseconds.

Emergency operating procedures

Immediate emergency message

Use 911 or the responsible agency directly when available. If radio is your usable path, call on a monitored, authorized frequency.

Say: EMERGENCY, EMERGENCY. This is AB9MZ. I need emergency assistance.

Give: exact location and access point; what happened; immediate hazards; number of people needing help; assistance needed; your callback or monitoring frequency.

Ask the receiving station to repeat the location and request. Record who accepted the message and when. Remain available for questions.

Do not assume that a calling frequency or repeater is monitored.

References: FCC rules; ICS forms

Field station first contact

1. Confirm a safe site, battery polarity, correct antenna and clear feed line. Start at low power.

2. Read back frequency, mode, power, offset and tone from the plan. Disable scan, unintended split, VOX and automatic transmit.

3. Listen first. Ask whether the frequency is in use. Give your call and request a brief radio check.

4. Exchange an actual message with the intended destination. Confirm intelligibility both ways and log the test.

5. If it fails, try the agreed alternate. Prove the path before starting the assignment.

Net check in and emergency break

Listen for net control and the requested check-in order. Avoid transmitting over another station.

Check-in: AB9MZ, Gregg, at Shelter One. One priority message for Operations. Battery station, available for two hours.

Emergency break: EMERGENCY, AB9MZ. Wait for acknowledgment, then give the essential facts.

For a directed net, obtain permission before changing frequency or leaving. If you lose contact, follow the written lost-communications plan.

Tactical names identify assignments. Give the required FCC call sign as well.

References: FCC rules; Message procedure


Voice and repeater operation

Plain language voice procedure

OVER: I am finished and expect a reply. OUT: this exchange is finished. Do not combine them.

SAY AGAIN: repeat the requested part. I SAY AGAIN: I am repeating. CORRECTION: replace the mistaken part that follows.

ROGER: I received your transmission. It does not mean yes or that the task is complete.

AFFIRMATIVE and NEGATIVE: yes and no. WAIT: pause briefly. READ BACK: repeat the message for verification.

Use plain language on incident voice nets. Speak slowly, pause for copying, and identify uncertain information.

References: Message procedure; ICS forms

Repeater programming

Record receive frequency and transmit frequency separately. Do not rely only on an offset symbol.

Common US examples: 2 meters 0.600 MHz; 1.25 meters 1.600 MHz; 70 centimeters 5.000 MHz. Direction and exceptions depend on the repeater.

CTCSS transmit tone opens access; receive tone squelch only controls what you hear. DCS codes and digital modes require the correct system settings.

Set FM or the required digital mode, bandwidth and power. Test briefly with your call sign. A courtesy tone or signal meter alone does not prove your voice reached the destination.

Repeater problem check

Hear nothing: verify volume, squelch, receive frequency, antenna and mode. Temporarily disable receive tone squelch.

Hear repeater but cannot access: verify transmit frequency, tone, power, battery under load and antenna. Check for busy lockout or transmit inhibit.

Repeater responds but nobody hears voice: check microphone selection, mic gain, digital versus analog mode and any external interface.

Leave pauses for emergency traffic, link delays and repeater timeout reset. Use the agreed simplex fallback when needed.

UTC and coordinates

Central Standard Time: add six hours to obtain UTC. Central Daylight Time: add five hours. Subtract for the reverse conversion; adjust the date at midnight.

Example: 2026-10-31 at 21:00 CDT is 2026-11-01 at 02:00 UTC. Do not assume a fixed daylight-saving offset all year.

Record 24-hour time and zone. Z means UTC. Say the date when midnight ambiguity matters.

For positions, state format and datum: decimal degrees, degrees and decimal minutes, or degrees minutes seconds. Read north or south and east or west explicitly.

US band privileges

Amateur rules before transmit

Confirm the control operator, license class, authorized band, mode, power and location restrictions. Keep the entire occupied signal within the permitted segment.

Identify at least every 10 minutes during a communication and at its end. Tactical identifiers do not replace your FCC call sign.

Use the minimum necessary power. Avoid harmful interference. Messages must not be encoded to obscure their meaning, subject to the specific rule exceptions.

Amateur authority does not grant routine access to public-safety, marine, aviation, CAP, MARS or SHARES channels.

References: FCC rules

How to read the band pages

Scope: FCC-regulated stations in the contiguous United States, ITU Region 2. Do not apply these pages unchanged in other countries or regions.

All HF numbers are MHz unless marked kHz. General, Advanced and Extra are separate license classes. Technician and legacy Novice have limited HF privileges.

CW may generally be used throughout a segment authorized to the operator. Data segments here mean RTTY and data; phone segments mean phone and image, with CW also permitted.

The listed endpoints are legal boundaries, not safe dial settings. Allow room for sidebands, drift and keying bandwidth.

References: FCC rules; Band privileges

Digital modes and linked systems

Digital voice and linked nodes

DMR: verify frequency, color code, time slot, talkgroup and contact type. D-STAR, System Fusion, P25 and other systems require their own mode and routing settings.

AllStarLink and EchoLink are network services, not RF modes. Their paths may depend on local power, a working node and internet.

Before linking, listen and identify. Check that the destination permits the connection. Use only documented commands for that particular node.

Prevent a stuck PTT or feedback loop. Test the local RF simplex path as an independent backup. Do not assume stored node numbers remain current.

FT8 and FT4 setup

Use the correct mode and frequency list in your installed software. Most HF operation uses USB or USB data even below 10 MHz.

Confirm call sign, grid, computer clock, audio input and output, CAT control and PTT. Aim for clock error well below one second.

Turn off speech processing. Set audio drive conservatively and follow the radio manual for ALC. Watch temperature and duty cycle.

FT8 alternates 15-second periods; FT4 uses 7.5-second periods. Verify the selected transmit period and audio offset. Keep Enable Tx off until ready.

Winlink in the field

Prepare the account, software, radio interface and a tested gateway or peer before departure. Download needed forms and channel information for offline use.

Gateway service often uses internet beyond the RF hop. Radio-only and peer-to-peer need their own compatible configuration; an ordinary internet email address is not a direct RF destination.

Choose the correct message type, connection mode and recipient. Keep messages short. Send a test and verify delivery with the recipient.

An outbox clearing or gateway acceptance is not recipient acknowledgment. Follow up on critical messages.

References: Winlink

APRS and position reporting

144.390 MHz FM is the customary terrestrial APRS frequency in North America. Confirm the local plan, path and mode before use.

Set the correct call sign and SSID, position, symbol, comment and sensible beacon rate. Avoid aggressive digipeater paths.

A transmitted packet is not proof it was received. A map report on the internet is not proof a field station can hear you.

For messages, request and verify acknowledgment where supported. State coordinate format and datum when passing a position by voice.

References: APRS

Mesh and satellite backups

Mesh: match hardware band, region, modem preset, channel and permitted security settings. Meshtastic, MeshCore and Reticulum are not automatically interoperable.

Test the whole message path and acknowledgment. A paired phone or visible neighbor does not prove destination reachability.

Part 15 operation and amateur licensed operation have different requirements. Do not mix amateur power privileges with encrypted amateur messages.

Satellite: verify current spacecraft status, uplink, downlink, mode, pass and Doppler plan. Hear the downlink first; use minimum necessary power. Never transmit blindly at a remembered satellite frequency.

Antennas power and station checks

Choose a useful HF band

For a regional path, 80 or 40 meters often deserves a first test. Daytime absorption, nighttime skip, season and interference can change the result.

For a longer path, test 20 meters and then higher or lower bands as conditions suggest. Higher HF bands can be excellent when open and unusable when closed.

NVIS uses a suitable low horizontal antenna and a band supported by current ionospheric conditions. It does not guarantee coverage.

Write primary and alternate bands into the plan. Test with the actual destination instead of assuming a propagation forecast proves the path.

Antenna length formulas

Starting estimates for wire antennas in free space; installation, wire insulation, surroundings and matching affect final length.

Half-wave dipole total feet = 468 divided by frequency in MHz.

Each dipole leg feet = 234 divided by frequency in MHz.

Quarter-wave vertical feet = 234 divided by frequency in MHz. It also needs a suitable return path or radial system.

Wavelength meters is approximately 300 divided by frequency in MHz. Cut long, measure safely, and trim gradually.

Antenna and feed line setup

Inspect connectors, insulators, strain relief and supports. Keep the antenna and its possible fall zone well away from power lines.

Use a suitable balun or choke where needed. Keep feed line bends gentle and protect connectors from water.

Mark guy lines and low wires with tactile and visible warnings. Keep walking routes clear and arrange assistance for unfamiliar sites.

Measure at low power. A tuner can match the transmitter while substantial feed-line loss remains. A low SWR does not prove good radiation or a safe RF exposure distance.

References: RF exposure; Electrical safety

Power planning

Average current = receive current times receive fraction, plus transmit current times transmit fraction.

Example: receive 0.7 A for 80 percent and transmit 8 A for 20 percent gives 2.16 A average.

A nominal 20 Ah battery with an assumed usable 16 Ah would give about 7.4 hours at 2.16 A before other losses. This is an estimate, not a runtime promise.

Allow margin for temperature, aging, voltage cutoff, accessories and longer transmissions. Measure current and loaded voltage during the actual mission profile.

DC power and batteries

Confirm the equipment voltage range and connector polarity before connection. Similar connectors may be wired differently.

Fuse the positive supply conductor near the battery according to cable capacity and equipment instructions. Protect exposed terminals and secure the battery.

Use a charger matched to the exact battery chemistry and temperature limits. Follow manufacturer limits for lithium charging, especially below freezing.

If voltage drops during transmit, inspect charge, wiring, connectors and cable size. Disconnect damaged, swelling, overheating or wet equipment safely.

Electrical and RF safety

Keep antennas, masts, tools and guy lines far enough from overhead wiring that no possible movement or fall can make contact. Obtain qualified help where clearance is uncertain.

Do not operate a generator indoors, in a garage or near doors and windows. Use a suitable outdoor location and follow its instructions.

Evaluate RF exposure for the actual antenna, power, duty cycle and public access. Do not use a universal distance rule.

Arrange lightning precautions before storms. Stop outdoor antenna work early; use a safe shelter and the site safety plan.

References: RF exposure; Electrical safety

No receive or no transmit

No receive: check power, volume, squelch, antenna, mode and frequency. Disable scan and receive tone filtering. Compare with a known signal.

No transmit: check inhibit, PTT selection, busy lockout, band limits, split, interface and battery voltage under load.

Carrier but no useful message: check microphone, audio routing, mode, digital settings, antenna and destination frequency.

Change one item at a time and log the result. Restore the last known working setup before trying the next branch. Switch to the alternate path before the reporting deadline.

Noise and interference

Compare antenna connected versus disconnected, then battery power versus external power. Change one variable at a time.

Disconnect suspected chargers, converters and USB devices individually. Keep power and data leads short; use appropriate ferrites and separation.

If nearby transmitters desensitize the receiver, increase separation and test filtering. More transmit power may worsen the shared-site problem.

For deliberate interference, avoid confrontation. Record time, frequency, mode, observations and any lawful recordings. Preserve facts and report through the appropriate coordinator or regulator.

References: FCC rules; Volunteer Monitor


Accessible deployment

Go kit check

Radio: programmed memories, microphone or key, antenna, feed line, adapters, spare fuses, manual and offline configuration backup.

Power: charged battery, safe leads, correct charger, distribution and a tested alternate.

Operating: this book, local channel plan, pencils, forms, clock, light, magnifier or accessible device, and spare charging leads.

Personal: identification, required credentials, water, food, clothing, medicines and accessibility equipment.

Before leaving: prove a two-way contact, confirm the assignment and tell your designated contact the return plan.

Accessible station setup

Place radio, power, controls and forms in a repeatable layout. Put cables away from cane routes, mobility aids and walking paths.

Label memories by function and place; add raised or braille identifiers where useful. Confirm that speech announces the actual frequency and mode.

Provide audio or text confirmation of PTT, power and alarms. Do not depend on a red LED or any color alone.

Use large print and glare control, with an accessible electronic backup. Agree on spoken readbacks and a buddy procedure for silent hazards or inaccessible displays.

Deployment and demobilization

Before travel: confirm assignment, site access, reporting location, supervisor, expected duration, personal needs and tested communications paths.

On arrival: check in; obtain the current channel and safety plans; verify a two-way path; start the log.

At relief: brief the next operator on pending messages, contacts, battery state, hazards and the last successful test.

Before leaving: obtain release, close or transfer open traffic, check out, secure equipment and notify your accountability contact.

Afterward: recharge, inspect, back up logs, document failures and update this book.


ARES

Amateur Radio Emergency Service is an ARRL public-service organization. Licensed amateurs register qualifications and equipment with local leadership.

Contact the local Emergency Coordinator. Ask about training, credentialing, assignments and the served-agency communications plan. ARRL membership is not required to apply.

For an activation: receive an assignment, check in, follow the incident organization, pass accurate messages and log your work.

ARES membership does not authorize self-deployment, entry into a restricted site, or transmission outside your license privileges.

References: ARES


RACES

Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service is a rule-defined civil-defense communications service, not simply another name for an ARES net.

Participating stations and control operators must meet 97.407 registration and certification requirements. Transmissions and contacts require the responsible civil-defense authorization.

Traffic is limited to the civil-defense purposes in the rule. Training normally totals no more than one hour per week, with specified state-approved exercise exceptions.

ARES and RACES roles can overlap locally, but enrollment, activation authority and permitted traffic must be confirmed separately.

References: RACES


MARS

Military Auxiliary Radio System supports Department of Defense contingency communications. It is separate from routine amateur operation.

Participation requires acceptance, training, assigned credentials and compliance with the applicable service program. Use only authorized frequencies, identifiers, modes and procedures.

An amateur license or a radio advertised as MARS capable is not sufficient authority. A wideband modification alone provides no operating permission.

Carry the current program-issued operating plan separately. Before switching services, verify identity, frequency, mode, power and authorization. Restore the amateur configuration when returning to amateur operation.

References: Army MARS


Civil Air Patrol

CAP uses its own authorized HF and VHF communications networks, including analog and digital systems. These are distinct from amateur bands.

Use current CAP training, qualifications, mission authorization and approved channel plans. Personally owned equipment must satisfy applicable NTIA and CAP requirements.

Do not substitute an amateur radio merely because it can tune to a CAP channel. Frequency coverage does not establish equipment compliance or operator authorization.

Keep the current mission channel plan with the radio. Use CAP-assigned identifiers and procedures when operating in that service.

References: Civil Air Patrol; CAP equipment


SHARES

Shared Resources High Frequency Radio Program coordinates existing HF capabilities for national-security and emergency-preparedness communications.

Participation is organizational and requires program enrollment and authorization. An amateur license alone does not confer access to SHARES channels.

Use the current program-issued directory, operational instructions, identifiers and equipment requirements. Record the sponsoring organization and responsible communications official in the deployment packet.

Treat SHARES and amateur operation as separate service configurations. Verify the full authorized setup before transmitting; do not use an internet frequency list as an operational assignment.

References: SHARES; SHARES overview


Official Observer and Volunteer Monitor

Historical term: Official Observer, or OO. The ARRL program closed in 2019 and was replaced by the Volunteer Monitor program.

Volunteer Monitors work within an ARRL and FCC arrangement. They observe operation and provide documented information; they are not FCC enforcement officers.

If you receive a notice, verify its source, review your station setup and records, and correct an identified technical or operating problem.

For interference reports, preserve times, frequencies, mode and factual observations. Do not argue on the air or claim authority you do not hold.

References: Volunteer Monitor


National Traffic System

NTS and message movement

National Traffic System moves formal messages through amateur traffic nets and relay stations. A radiogram preserves the originator, destination and exact text across those relays.

Find the local net, practice before deployment, and ask net control where to route traffic. Nets can connect local, section, regional and wider paths.

Accept a message only when you can copy it accurately and arrange onward relay or delivery. Record each handoff.

An accepted message is not necessarily delivered. Track critical traffic through the required recipient confirmation and service reports.

References: Traffic system; Message procedure; Delivery standards

Radiogram preamble

Record in order: message number; precedence; optional HX instructions; station of origin; check; place of origin; optional filing time; date.

Then record the addressee and delivery information, the text, and the signature of the person originating the message.

The station of origin is the station first placing the message into radiogram form, not necessarily the person who wrote it.

The check counts text groups only. Exclude preamble, address and signature. Preserve the original number and station of origin through relays.

References: Message procedure

Radiogram worked example

Practice message, not an actual incident.

Preamble: 1 R AB9MZ 10 TINLEY PARK IL 0200Z NOV 1

To: EXERCISE CONTROL. Use the destination contact supplied by your exercise.

Text: EXERCISE SHELTER ONE HAS POWER X NO ADDITIONAL BATTERIES NEEDED

Signature: GREGG

Check is 10: EXERCISE 1, SHELTER 2, ONE 3, HAS 4, POWER 5, X 6, NO 7, ADDITIONAL 8, BATTERIES 9, NEEDED 10.

References: Message procedure

Message precedence

EMERGENCY: immediate life-safety urgency. Spell out EMERGENCY in the preamble; do not abbreviate it to E.

P, Priority: important time-sensitive emergency-related traffic that does not meet Emergency precedence.

W, Welfare: health-and-welfare inquiries or advisories concerning people in a disaster area. R, Routine: ordinary traffic.

Current NTS 2.0 material also provides Certified handling for qualifying routine messages requiring confirmed delivery within 48 hours. Use the current origination procedure; do not invent a precedence code.

The originator determines the appropriate urgency. Do not upgrade routine traffic to obtain faster service.

References: Message procedure; Delivery standards

Handling instructions A through F

Current ARRL and RRI meanings, revised 2025-11-16. Do not use older cards for HXA.

HXA: email or text delivery preferred.

HXB: cancel if not delivered within the specified hours; service the origin.

HXC: report delivery date and time to the origin.

HXD: every handling station reports receive and relay or delivery details to the origin. Avoid during overloaded disaster circuits.

HXE: obtain a reply and originate it back. HXF: hold delivery until the specified date.

References: Current HX codes

Handling instructions G through T

HXG: if delivery involves expense, cancellation is permitted; service the origin.

HXI: deliver as a radiogram-ICS-213 message.

HXR: confirm the addressee has read the message and reply to the originator. Reserved for Emergency, Priority and Certified messages.

HXT: special traffic-test message, with the specified test identifier.

Keep parameters, combinations and net instructions with the message. For HXC and HXE, an addressee receiving directly on the air is responsible for the reply.

References: Current HX codes

Copy send and deliver traffic

Copy exactly. Ask for fills before acknowledging. Use SAY AGAIN WORD AFTER, WORD BEFORE, or the requested group as your net procedure permits.

Send at the receiving operator's copying speed. Spell ambiguous names and read numbers separately. Use breaks to separate address, text and signature.

Read back critical locations, quantities and instructions. Log received-from, sent-to, UTC times and message number.

For delivery, identify yourself, verify the recipient and read the exact message and signature. Record delivery or failure and send the required service message. Never fabricate a missing word.

References: Message procedure; Delivery standards

Numbered texts and ICS messages

ARL numbered radiograms refer to standardized text. Use the current official list at both ends and fill all required blanks. An ARL-prefixed check counts the transmitted text groups.

Do not guess an ARL number from memory or substitute an old list during a real incident. Plain-text messages avoid a mismatched lookup table.

ICS 213 is a general-message form; a radiogram is a traffic transport format. They are not interchangeable without an agreed mapping.

For radiogram-ICS-213 traffic, follow the current NTS encoding procedure and use HXI when required.

References: Message procedure; Current HX codes; ICS forms

Storm spotter report

Protect yourself first. Report from safe shelter or an approved observation point. Do not approach a tornado, floodwater, downed wiring or unstable debris.

Give: what you observed; exact location; observation time and zone; whether still occurring; measured versus estimated size or speed; your call sign and callback.

Distinguish direct observation from a relayed report. Never turn an estimate into a measured value.

Use the current local NWS reporting criteria and route. Chicago serves much of northeastern Illinois; Milwaukee Sullivan serves much of southern Wisconsin. Verify the responsible office for the actual site.

References: Storm spotting; Chicago weather; Milwaukee weather
